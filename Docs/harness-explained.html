Where numbers come from

Faida's whole promise is that you can believe the number. That works today because every number sits right next to the photo it came from. We are about to start making numbers you cannot look at — and there is one small thing to fix first.

This is the short, visual version. The longer memo has the code details and the full argument.

01 A number moves away from its photo

Follow one figure — AED 402.00 off a real invoice — as the product grows.

Everything Faida does today lands in the first two columns. The owner forwards a photo, gets a number back, and if he doubts it he looks at the picture. That is the entire basis of trust in the product, and so far it has been enough.

The next two milestones move us into the other two columns. A cost per kilo is one division away from the paper. A plate's margin is four. By the time the screen says "this biryani earns you AED 6.40", there is no photograph that shows you that.

You can only trust a number you cannot see if the system can tell you what went into it.

02 Two numbers that look identical

This is the whole problem, as small as I can make it.

Neither number is wrong. The second one is the right thing to do — the plan already says so: when the total is off the edge of the photo, ask rather than guess. The problem is only that afterwards the two look identical.

That does not matter much while an invoice is a page you can pull up. It matters a great deal once that 402.00 has been divided twice and is quietly sitting inside a plate cost, because nothing downstream can tell that this particular biryani's margin rests on something somebody remembered rather than something a camera saw.

The same problem, one size larger. Next milestone we start merging supplier items into single ingredients — Al Madina's milk powder and Gulf Foods' milk powder becoming one thing you cook with. A person approves each merge, one keystroke.

Get one of those merges wrong and the cost of every dish using that ingredient is quietly wrong. And unlike a misread invoice, there is no photo to check it against. Today we would not even be able to say who approved it.

03 We are not the first to hit this

Four rules, and how Faida already scores against them.

The picture you sent is an accounting company explaining how they built software that lets machines do real work with real consequences. Take the accounting words out and their advice is four rules. I checked all four against our actual code. We already follow three — not because anyone copied them, but because they fall out of the rules we wrote after the last build went wrong.

Tidy it at the door

Nothing downstream should ever have to squint at a photo. Sort out the mess once, on the way in.

Ours is the strongest part of the build. The awkward things — which currency, which unit, how big the pack is, cash or credit — are all decided by rules we can test, not by how nicely the prompt is worded. Every one of those got moved into code after it got something wrong.

✓ We do this

Keep one set of books

One place holds the truth, and one piece of code decides what is correct. Two copies drift, and then you spend your life reconciling them.

We learned this the hard way in August: our exam had a private copy of the "does this invoice add up" rule, it drifted, and it spent a day marking correct answers wrong. We deleted the copy.

✓ We do this

One door for everyone

A person and a machine should not be able to quietly disagree. Both go through the same check, or somebody has built a side door.

This one surprised me. Fixing a field on the review screen does not have its own code — it runs the exact same function as texting "line 4 qty 16" into WhatsApp, and typing an invoice in by hand runs the same checks as a photo.

✓ We do this

Know who did it, before you let anything act alone

Anything you cannot trace, you cannot safely hand over. Write down who did what first; widen what the machine is allowed to do afterwards.

We do half of this, and it is the wrong half. We keep a detailed record of everything the model did. We keep nothing about what a person did.

✕ This is the gap

Worth saying out loud: three out of four, in working code, is a good place to be. The last build had a hundred and thirty thousand lines and never sent a single real WhatsApp message. This one has under five thousand and already gets the architecture mostly right.

04 Three small fixes

None of these is a new system. Two are a column and a table.

1 · Save one extra word next to every number

About half a day · do it before the raw-materials work starts

Every stored figure also records how it got there: read off the page, re-read on a second look, corrected in chat, corrected on the screen, told to us because it was missing, or typed in by hand — plus who and when.

total  402.00

With the fix

It also settles a question the plan left open: whether a plain "OK" should be allowed to confirm an invoice whose total we never read. It should not — a missing total is invisible to everything downstream, while one we asked for is labelled and can be trusted for what it is.

2 · Start keeping a note of who approved what — now, not in two months

About two hours · currently scheduled two milestones too late

Confirmations, corrections, and above all the merging of two suppliers' items into one ingredient, each with a name against it.

Today
milk powder  merged
by  —
→
With the fix
milk powder  merged
by  the consultant · 3 Sept

The objection is that we have no logins until later, so we do not know who anyone is. True, and it does not matter. "The consultant at the console", or the phone number that sent the message, is a real answer where today the answer is silence. When real logins arrive, the name simply gets more precise.

3 · A calculated number is never safer than its shakiest ingredient

A written rule · costs nothing now · saves a rebuild later

We already say a dish with one uncosted ingredient must read incomplete, never as a cheap dish. This widens that from missing to shaky: if a plate's cost leans on a pack size we could not read, or a total we had to ask for, the plate says so — and says which ingredient dragged it down.

05 Done — this is now how it works

Shipped 28 August

All three, in front of M5.

Every number a photo produces now records that a camera saw it. Every number a person types — in chat, on the screen, or into the manual form — records that a person said it, and which person. Fields nobody touched keep the model's reading, so a one-field correction marks one field and nothing else.

Every confirmation, correction and hand-entered invoice now leaves a line saying who did it, written in the same breath as the change itself — so an invoice that is confirmed but has nobody's name against it is not a thing that can exist. Material merges will write the same line the moment that screen is built.

The rule about calculated numbers is written down and waiting: nothing yet computes a cost per kilo, so there is nothing yet to apply it to. It is there so the first thing that does inherits it, rather than us discovering the need after a margin screen shows a confident number built on a guess.

The thing I did not do is treat this as a rewrite. Three of the four rules already held. The job was to notice that, write down the one that was missing, and get back to costing a plate of biryani — which is what happens next.

Everything said here about how Faida works today was read from the code on 28 August, not from the plan — where the two disagree, the code is right. The four rules come from Rillet's own description of their product; their customer numbers are the company's own and nothing here depends on them.
